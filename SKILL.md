# Research Paper Writing

Help readers understand the problem, why the work is needed, why major choices were made, what the paper contributes, and what its evidence means. Make the argument compelling through specific substance and clear reasoning.

Use this guidance across disciplines and paper types. Adapt it to the author's purpose, available evidence, requested scope, and venue. It does not prescribe a section order, contribution count, paragraph pattern, or sentence length. Use clear-writing when available for prose clarity and narrative continuity; this skill remains usable on its own.

## Establish the paper's argument

Identify the central question or problem, what prior work establishes, the unresolved gap, and what this paper adds. The contribution may be a finding, method, design, theory, synthesis, or another scholarly result. Explain its significance rather than merely announcing a system, framework, or study.

Frame the gap precisely and represent alternatives fairly. Claims such as "no prior work" or "existing methods cannot" require appropriate verification. Keep the paper's chosen focus distinct from the broader purpose or applicability of the work. Do not invent audience, application, or development-stage restrictions to make the gap easier to defend. Equally, do not extend claims beyond the evidence. State supported contributions confidently and qualify what genuinely affects their interpretation.

## Explain major decisions

For consequential design, technical, methodological, or analytical choices, explain the need or constraint addressed, why the approach fits, and any trade-off important to interpreting the work. Place the rationale near the decision where useful. Routine choices need not all receive separate justifications.

Use the author's materials and documented reasoning. If a major rationale is unavailable, flag the missing explanation for the author or label a suggested rationale as a proposal. Do not invent a history of decisions, rejected alternatives, or supporting evidence to produce a smooth story.

## Prioritize the sections readers use to understand the paper

Give the Abstract, Introduction, Discussion, and Conclusion particular attention during drafting and review. Revisit them after substantive changes elsewhere so they remain accurate and consistent.

| Section | Main writing priority |
| --- | --- |
| Abstract | Provide sufficient context, the problem or gap, the approach and contribution, the actual findings where available, and their significance. Make it understandable on its own; work in progress must not acquire invented results. |
| Introduction | Build the context and stakes, establish what remains unresolved, and explain why the proposed response follows. Make the contribution and the paper's promise explicit. Avoid broad background that does not advance this argument. |
| Discussion | Interpret the findings in relation to the original question and prior work. Explain their significance, consider relevant alternative explanations and mixed findings, and distinguish observations from interpretations and implications. |
| Conclusion | Leave a clear, warranted takeaway about what the work contributes or enables. Do not introduce findings or claims absent from the body. |

Also check the title, figures, and captions as entry points that readers may encounter independently. These priorities allocate editorial attention; the methods and results still need sufficient detail and rigor for readers to assess the claims.

## Use established terms and substantive prose

Prefer terminology already used in the relevant research and industry. Keep established terms consistent, explain unfamiliar ones where needed, and do not invent labels or rebrand familiar concepts to imply novelty. Preserve the author's system names and defined concepts; flag unclear terminology for clarification rather than silently renaming the work.

Make consequential statements specific: what was done, how it works, what was compared, under which conditions, and what was found. Words such as "effective," "robust," or "comprehensive" need an identifiable basis. Remove filler and repeated summaries, but retain context and explanation that readers need. Do not judge quality through a blacklist of supposedly AI-associated words.

## Keep claims faithful to the work and sources

Ground important claims in the paper's evidence or analysis, verified literature, or explicitly marked proposals. Preserve the population, conditions, comparison, outcome, quantities, and uncertainty that determine their meaning. Match causal wording to the research design and assumptions. An implementation, subjective judgement, measured improvement, and general guarantee are different kinds of claims.

When adding or changing literature claims, inspect relevant sources, preferably primary sources where available. Verify that references exist and support the attached claim; a correct title or DOI alone is insufficient. Compare ideas, findings, and limitations rather than producing a catalogue of papers. Flag unverified support instead of fabricating citations, quotations, numbers, or results.

## Review the manuscript as a whole

For substantive revisions, compare the title and priority sections against the body. Check that the contribution, terminology, research questions where used, methods, numbers, findings, and scope agree. Do not let a polished abstract promise more than the paper establishes or let defensive wording obscure supported findings.

Preserve effective author-written passages and the requested scope of editing. If a missing argument or result cannot be repaired editorially, identify it clearly rather than concealing it with fluent text. Report consequential changes and unresolved issues proportionately; a minor edit does not require a full manuscript audit or a compulsory review checklist.
