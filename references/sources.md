# Sources and how to use them

The core skill combines editorial guidance with research on the fidelity of AI-generated scientific summaries. These sources support the principles; they do not establish a universal paper structure or characterize every model and writing task. Apply discipline- and venue-specific requirements when relevant, checking current instructions rather than treating a guide's examples as rules.

## Research-writing guidance

- **Brett Mensh and Konrad Kording (2017), "Ten simple rules for structuring papers."** [PLOS Computational Biology](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619), 13(9), e1005619. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619. Supports a clear central contribution, sufficient context, logical progression, interpreting the findings, and attention to titles, abstracts, and figures. Its particular structural prescriptions are editorial advice, not required templates for every genre.
- **Simon Peyton Jones, "How to Write a Great Research Paper."** [Author's page and slides](https://simon.peytonjones.org/great-research-paper/). Supports explicit contributions, connecting claims with evidence, explaining ideas through concrete examples, fair treatment of prior work, and feedback from readers. The slide deck's page counts, abstract length, and categorical style advice are contextual and are not adopted as universal rules.

## Research on AI-assisted meaning drift

- **Uwe Peters and Benjamin Chin-Yee (2025), "Generalization bias in large language model summarization of scientific research."** [Royal Society Open Science, open-access text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12042776/), 12(4), 241776. DOI: 10.1098/rsos.241776. Compared 4,900 model-generated summaries with source texts and documented broader generalizations in many tested outputs. This supports checking preservation of scope and qualifiers. It does not establish that every simplification or general statement is invalid, or that all models behave identically.
- **Hye Sun Yun et al. (2025), "Caught in the Web of Words: Do LLMs Fall for Spin in Medical Literature?"** [Proceedings of the sixth Conference on Health, Inference, and Learning](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v287/yun25a.html), PMLR 287, 458-479. Tested 22 models' susceptibility to positive framing in medical-trial literature and found that generated summaries could propagate that framing. This motivates checking interpretations against actual outcomes, including mixed findings. The experimental setting is medical literature; general paper-writing recommendations are an editorial application of that evidence.

Reference existence and accurate bibliographic details do not establish support for a particular claim. Inspect the relevant source content when using it in a manuscript. These references are available guidance, not citations to insert automatically into papers using the skill.
